Choosing a tool

What password managers can't do: tell you who's on the other end

Bitwarden Send, 1Password sharing and the one-time secret sites all encrypt properly. Every comparison of them measures the same thing — and it isn't the thing that decides whether your secret lands in the right hands.

The 15-second answer: if you already pay for a password manager, use its sharing feature. Bitwarden Send and 1Password's item sharing are both properly encrypted and both good enough. Picking between them matters far less than the step nearly everyone skips.

That step is confirming, through some channel other than the one that asked, that the person receiving the link is who you think. No sharing tool can do that part for you.

Every comparison measures the container

Search for a way to send a password and you'll find the same article about a dozen times over, usually published by one of the tools being compared. The scoring criteria are always roughly these: is the encryption done on your device or on the server, does it self-destruct after one view, can you set an expiry, do you need an account, can you self-host it, and what does the free tier allow.

Those are reasonable questions. They are also, all six of them, questions about the container — how well the box is sealed and how quickly it dissolves. Not one of them asks who is holding the box when it opens.

That framing is inherited from the threat these tools were built for. The original problem was a password sitting in a Slack thread or an email archive for six years. Expiring encrypted links solve that problem, genuinely and well. But solving it exposes what was underneath it.

What these tools genuinely do well

Worth being specific, because the criticism below is narrow and I don't want it read as a general dismissal. These are good pieces of software.

Bitwarden Send generates a key on your device and encrypts with AES-256 before anything is uploaded, so what the server stores is ciphertext it has no way to read. You can set an expiry, cap the number of views, and add a separate password. The sender needs an account; the recipient just opens a link. On the free tier it handles text, with files reserved for paid plans.

1Password's item sharing does the same job from inside the vault, with expiry options and a view-once setting, and it has one capability the others don't — more on that in a moment.

The standalone one-time link sites are the fastest route when you don't want an account at all. The one distinction worth understanding: some encrypt in your browser, and some receive your secret in plaintext and encrypt it server-side. Both will describe themselves as secure, and for the second kind the operator handles the secret in the clear on the way in. If you're using a hosted service with no account, it's worth thirty seconds checking which kind it is.

All of them beat pasting a password into a chat window. If this article persuades you of nothing else, use any of them.

The question none of them answer

Here is the failure that survives all of this, and it doesn't involve anyone breaking encryption.

An email arrives from your bookkeeper's real address, in the real thread, asking for the payroll login. You do the responsible thing: you don't paste it into the reply. You create a properly encrypted, view-once link that expires in an hour, and you send the link back in that thread.

The mailbox is compromised. The link works exactly as designed. It encrypts on your device, the server never sees the contents, it self-destructs after a single view, and the one view belongs to someone else. Every feature on the comparison chart performed correctly.

Encryption protects the secret in transit. It has nothing to say about the address. A sealed envelope delivered to the wrong door is still a sealed envelope, and the seal is the part these tools are measured on.

The lookalike-domain version fails identically: one character different, you type the address yourself, and the tool faithfully protects the message all the way to a stranger.

This is not a hypothetical class of attack. Invoice and payment-redirection fraud — the family the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center files under business email compromise — works precisely this way. There is no cryptography to defeat, because the attacker isn't attacking the cryptography. They're attacking the assumption sitting underneath it, which is that you know who you're talking to.

The one that gets closest

1Password deserves credit here, because it's the only mainstream option that treats the recipient as a thing to be checked rather than assumed. You can restrict a shared item to specific email addresses, and anyone opening it has to verify that address with a one-time code before the contents appear.

That's a real control and it's better than nothing by a wide margin. It stops a forwarded link working for whoever it was forwarded to, which is the most common accidental leak.

But notice exactly what it proves. It proves whoever opened the link can read mail at the address you typed. If you took that address from the message that made the request, you've just confirmed the attacker controls the mailbox they were already sending from. The check is real; it is checking the wrong side of the problem.

The distinction worth keeping: proving someone controls an account is authentication. Proving the account belongs to the person you mean is identification. Sharing tools are getting good at the first one. Nobody can do the second for you from inside the message.

What actually settles it, starting with free

The fix isn't a better sharing tool. It's one step before the sharing tool, and the best versions cost nothing.

1. Confirm on a channel you chose

Call the person on a number you already had — your contacts, a previous invoice, the firm's site typed in yourself. Not a number in the signature, and not a reply asking "is this really you?", which whoever sent it will cheerfully confirm. This is free, takes a minute, and defeats the entire scenario above. If you take one thing from this page, take this one.

2. Agree something in advance

For people you deal with repeatedly, a phrase agreed beforehand removes the improvisation. It doesn't need to be elaborate, it needs to exist before you need it. The same idea works for family and for a bookkeeper you send payroll details to twice a month. We wrote about the family version in detail in the piece on bank calls.

3. Use an app that verifies keys, and check the verification

Signal has safety numbers you can compare in person or over a call you placed. Doing that once, properly, gives you a channel where you know who's at the other end and the messages are strongly encrypted. It's free, it's open source, its cryptography is excellent, and for two people willing to install the same app it is a completely legitimate answer to this problem. The catch is the one everyone knows: the verification screen exists, and almost nobody opens it.

Do this this week

  1. Stop optimising the tool choice. If you have a password manager, its share feature is fine. If you don't, any of the browser-encrypting one-time link sites will do. This decision is not where your risk is.
  2. Add one rule: the request and the confirmation never travel on the same channel. If it came by email, confirm by phone. If it came by phone, confirm by a number you sourced yourself.
  3. Write down the numbers for the three or four people you send sensitive things to — accountant, bookkeeper, landlord, whoever — somewhere that isn't your inbox. The reason people skip the call-back is that finding the number takes effort at the exact moment they're in a hurry.
  4. Treat any change of bank details as a phone call, always. No exceptions, however ordinary the email looks. This single rule covers most of what invoice fraud relies on.
  5. If you use Signal with someone, verify the safety numbers. Once, today, then never again.

Where this leaves us

Everything above is free and needs nothing from me. The remaining gap is narrow: the call-back works beautifully for one-off situations, and a code word works for people you know well, but neither scales comfortably to a handful of contacts you exchange sensitive details with every week.

That's the case SimplyAuth was built for. It shows both people a matching phrase on screen at the same moment, so identity is settled inside the exchange rather than in a separate phone call, and then sends the information end-to-end encrypted with an expiry. It requires both sides to have the app, which is a real cost and the reason the free methods come first on this page.

I'm Kevin — I build SimplyAuth, which is a competitor to some of the tools discussed above, so read this with that in mind. I've tried to be accurate about what they do well, because the encryption criticism people expect me to make isn't the one I have: Bitwarden and 1Password encrypt properly, and I'm not claiming better cryptography than Signal, which is free and excellent. My argument is narrower — that identity is a separate question from encryption, and the comparison articles don't ask it.
