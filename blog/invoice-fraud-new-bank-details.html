Paying people safely

The invoice email said the bank details changed

The expensive version of invoice fraud arrives from your supplier's real email address, in a thread you started. Checking the sender tells you nothing. Here is what settles it instead.

The 15-second answer: hold the payment, and don't reply to the email to check. Phone the supplier on a number you already had before the request arrived — from the signed contract or an invoice you have already paid — and ask them to tell you the new account number. Don't read it out to them for a yes or no.

If you can't find a number that predates the email, you don't have a way to verify this yet. That is the real problem, and there's a section on it below.

Why "check the sender's address" isn't enough

Most writing on invoice fraud is a list of warning signs: a lookalike domain, a changed signature, pressure to pay today. Those signs are real, and they catch the cheap version of this scam, where somebody registers a domain one character off your supplier's and hopes nobody looks closely.

They do not catch the version that actually empties accounts. In that one, nothing is faked at all. The criminal is inside the supplier's genuine mailbox. The email arrives from the correct address, in a thread you started, under a subject line you wrote, attached to the invoice you were already expecting to pay. Every technical check your mail system can run comes back clean, because the message really is from the account it claims to be from. SPF, DKIM and DMARC confirm a message came from the right server; none of them were designed to confirm what the sender wants.

This is why business email compromise lands hard on small businesses specifically. There is often no second approver, and the person paying the invoice is the person who negotiated the work. The request also turns up at the exact moment a payment is genuinely due, which is no coincidence: whoever is reading that mailbox has been waiting for it.

Replying to the email is the one thing not to do

Replying feels like diligence. It is the most common way people talk themselves into paying.

If the mailbox is compromised, your confirmation request goes to the criminal — and they will confirm it. Hijacked accounts also tend to have a quiet filing or forwarding rule set on them, so your message can be moved out of the inbox before the genuine supplier ever sees that you asked.

A fast, friendly reply explaining that they've moved banks isn't reassurance either. The person answering has full sight of your conversation and every reason to sound helpful.

So the rule is the same as for a phone call claiming to be your bank: confirmation has to happen on a channel other than the one the request arrived on.

How to make a call-back actually prove something

Phoning the supplier is the right answer. It is free and it needs no software. But "call to confirm" is advice that can be followed badly, so the details carry the weight.

  1. Get the number from before the request. A signed contract, an invoice you have already paid, your own vendor records, or the supplier's website reached by typing the address in yourself. Not the signature on the new email, not a number in the attachment, not a link in the thread.
  2. Call. Don't email or text. If you reach a general line, ask for your usual contact by name.
  3. Make them state the details. This is the step most people get backwards. "Can I just check, have you moved to account 1234 5678?" can be answered correctly by anyone who says yes. Ask instead: "I've had a request to change your payment details — can you read me the new account number?" Then compare it yourself.
  4. Ask the easy questions. Who authorised the change, when it takes effect, which outstanding invoices it covers, and what the previous account was. A genuine supplier answers all four without effort.
  5. Write down what happened. Who confirmed it, when, and which number you rang. Update the vendor record only after that call.

When you have no number that predates the email

This is the case the guides skip, and for a small business it is often the normal one. Your supplier may be one person, and the only number you have ever had for them might be the one in their email signature — which is to say, the one whoever controls the mailbox can edit.

Some genuine routes to a number you can trust:

  • Older paperwork: a quote, a contract, a delivery note, a card receipt, a calendar invitation.
  • Their website, reached by typing the address yourself rather than following a link in the email.
  • A public company register. In many countries these list a registered address and named officers, which is enough to find a second route in.
  • A human bridge: your accountant, or anyone else who works with the same supplier, may have a number on file.
  • A channel that isn't the mailbox at all, such as a message to a named person on a professional network.

If none of that works, be honest that you cannot verify the change rather than paying and hoping. Two things still help:

Pay the old account. This is the most underused move available, and it is free. If the request is fraudulent, your money goes where it was always going and nothing is lost. If the change is genuine, the payment bounces or the supplier chases you — and now you are talking to the real supplier about a payment they expect, which is the conversation you wanted. The exception is if you have already confirmed through a trusted channel that the old account is closed.

Let the deadline be the test. Say plainly that you can't release funds to new details until you've confirmed them by phone, and ask when is good to call.

A real supplier is not insulted by being verified. They are usually relieved, because they lose both the money and the customer when this works. If a request to change payment details treats a five-minute phone call as an offence, that reaction is the most reliable signal in this piece.

Make it a rule so nobody has to be clever

The vulnerability isn't ignorance. It is a judgement call made by one busy person against a deadline. Writing the rule down removes the judgement, and costs nothing:

  • No change to payment details on the strength of an email, ever, from anyone.
  • New details sit pending for a set number of days before anything is paid to them.
  • A second person confirms the call took place. In a two-person company, that is the other person.
  • Call-back numbers come from the vendor file, and the vendor file is updated only after a verified call — never from an email signature.

The other direction: when your details are the ones changing

Nobody writes this side of it, and one day it is you. If your details genuinely change, you have to make something that looks exactly like fraud believable to people correctly told never to trust such an email.

What works is deciding it in advance. Tell customers at the outset that you will never change payment details by email alone, and put a phone number on the contract, where it can't be edited later. When the change is real, say so on more than one channel, state the previous account, give an effective date, and ask them to ring the contract number to confirm. Then be gracious when they make you prove it.

Do this this week

  1. Send the one-line rule to whoever pays your invoices: no payment detail changes from email, no exceptions, call first.
  2. Fill in the phone numbers in your vendor records now, while nothing is urgent, taking them from contracts rather than signatures.
  3. Turn on your bank's controls. Dual authorisation, alerts on new payees, account name checking and limits on first payments to a new payee are standard on business accounts and widely left switched off.
  4. Decide who you phone for a second opinion before you need one.
  5. If you have already paid: call your bank immediately and ask for a recall, because the odds fall away by the hour. Tell the real supplier, keep the original email with its headers, and report it — in the US to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov and to the FTC, and elsewhere to your national cybercrime body.

Once you know who you're dealing with

Verifying the person is the first half. The details still have to travel, and they usually travel as an email attachment or a text that sits in a mailbox indefinitely. The principle holds whatever you use: send them through something that encrypts the contents and doesn't keep them afterwards. The one-time link features in the major password managers do this well, and many people already pay for one.
