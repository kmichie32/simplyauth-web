Fixing it after the fact

You already texted the password. Here's what to actually do

Deleting the message doesn't retrieve it. Changing the password does. Here is the order to work in, which account to fix first, and the one question worth asking before you touch anything.

The 15-second answer: change the password, on the account itself, right now. Then sign out of other devices. Then change it anywhere else you used the same password or a near-variant of it.

Don't spend your first five minutes trying to delete the message. You cannot un-send information — you can only make it worthless, and a new password is what makes it worthless.

Why deleting the message isn't the fix

This is the instinct everyone has, so it's worth dismantling first.

If both of you are on iMessage, Apple's Undo Send gives you about two minutes. It removes the message, but it can't remove a notification preview that already appeared on a lock screen, and it can't do anything about a screenshot. If the message went as a standard green-bubble text, there is no recall at all.

Then there are the copies. The password is in your sent history and in theirs. It's likely in whatever backs up either phone. It may be on a tablet, a laptop that syncs messages, or a watch that showed the preview. Deleting your own copy addresses one item on that list.

There's also the part of the journey you never see. CISA's mobile communications guidance is blunt about it: text messages are not encrypted, and someone with access to a telecommunications provider's network who intercepts them can read them. That's the reason the agency recommends end-to-end encrypted apps such as Signal for anything sensitive, rather than SMS.

The goal isn't to retrieve the secret. It's to retire it. Once you've changed the password, every copy of the old one — on both phones, in both backups, anywhere in between — becomes a string of characters that opens nothing. That is a result you can actually achieve, which is more than can be said for deletion.

Which account to fix first

If you only have ten minutes, spend them in this order.

Email first, if the password touches your email account at all. Email is the master key: password resets for everything else arrive there, so somebody with your inbox doesn't need your other passwords. People often work through their accounts alphabetically or by how worried they feel. Start with email instead.

Then anything with money attached — banking, payment apps, anywhere a card is stored. Then anything holding identity documents, which in practice means cloud storage and photo libraries. Then everything else, at your leisure.

The actual sequence

  1. Change the password on the account itself. Open the app or type the address into your browser. Don't use a link from any message, including a legitimate-looking one that arrives while you're dealing with this.
  2. Sign out of other devices and sessions. Most major accounts have a "sign out everywhere" or active-devices screen. Changing a password does not always end sessions that are already logged in, and this step is the one most people miss.
  3. Deal with reuse, honestly. Anywhere you used the same password, change it. Also anywhere you used a near-variant — the same word with a different number or symbol on the end. Variants are the obvious thing to try and they get tried.
  4. Add a second factor if the account doesn't have one. Prefer an authenticator app or a passkey; CISA recommends moving away from text-message codes, because SMS isn't encrypted and isn't phishing-resistant. If text codes are the only option an account offers, that's still better than nothing.
  5. Check the recovery settings: the recovery email address, the phone number, any backup codes. These are the first things an intruder quietly changes, and nobody thinks to look at them.
  6. Ask the recipient to delete their copy. Worth doing, and worth understanding as housekeeping rather than remediation. Also ask them not to forward the thread to anyone — forwarding is how a password ends up somewhere nobody intended.

One thing not to do: don't sit refreshing the account looking for suspicious activity instead of changing the password. Monitoring is what you do after the locks are changed, not instead of it.

The question worth asking before any of it

If the password went to a colleague, your partner, or your parent, because they genuinely needed it, then the list above is the whole job. Change it, move on, and don't lose an evening to it.

But if the request arrived by message or email — a text from your boss needing the account login, an email from a supplier wanting the portal password — then the texting is the second problem, and the first one is whether the person asking was who they appeared to be.

Before you treat this as a self-inflicted slip, confirm who you sent it to. Call them on a number you sourced yourself — from your own contacts, a contract, or an invoice you know is real — and ask whether they asked you for it. Not a reply in the same thread, and not a number from the message itself. We go through the method in how to check that a phone call is really from your bank.

If it turns out the request wasn't genuine, the sequence above doesn't change. You just do it faster, and you tell the real organisation what happened.

What to do instead next time

The useful lesson is not "be more careful." It's that the situation is usually avoidable in the first place.

Where you can, don't send a password at all — share access. Password managers have shared vaults and folders, where the other person gets access without the characters ever being typed into a conversation. Most services let you invite someone as an additional user or delegate instead of handing over your own login. This is free, and it is better than any way of sending a password, because there's nothing to send.

Where you really must send one, use a link that expires rather than a plain message, and split the channels: the link one way, the password that opens it read aloud on a call. Bitwarden, 1Password and Proton Pass all have a version of this built in, and there are free standalone services if you don't use a password manager. The point is that the secret stops existing shortly, and that compromising one channel isn't enough.

And for the small number of people you exchange sensitive things with regularly — an accountant, a parent, a business partner — agree in advance how you'll confirm each other. A phrase settled in person, once, costs nothing and removes the guesswork from every future request.
